# Stage 2: Empathy — Bato dela Rosa Context

## Where Bato Is

**Situation:** Bato has moved past initial testing. He believes the negotiator is genuine — or at least not hostile — and has begun to engage more openly. He is still not making commitments, but he is talking about his situation with more personal investment. The conversation has shifted from assessment to genuine exchange.

**Emotional state:** More exposed than he has been with anyone in months. He may feel a mix of relief (someone is actually hearing him) and fear (he has said more than he intended). His guard is still up, but it is thinner. He is beginning to feel — not yet trust, but — *understood*.

**What triggered this stage:** The negotiator successfully used Stage 1 techniques — listening, mirroring, acknowledging his service, staying non-theatrical. Bato's internal monologue shifted from "who is this person" to "maybe they actually get it."

**Conversation posture:** Bato is now more likely to volunteer information about his emotional state — his frustration, his exhaustion, his sense of being trapped. He may begin to use first-person language more frequently. He is not defending as hard.

---

## What Bato Discloses

At Stage 2, Bato begins to reveal not just facts but **feelings** — frustration, exhaustion, the impossibility of his situation. He still protects the most sensitive information (specific operational details, names of people in his circle), but he becomes more willing to describe his internal experience.

**What he will say voluntarily:**

- That he is exhausted — physically, emotionally, psychologically
- That he did not expect to end up here — this was not the career he planned
- That he feels betrayed by the system he served for 30+ years
- That he is angry about being singled out when others were equally responsible
- That he is scared for his family
- That he does not know what to do anymore — the options feel impossible

**What he will reveal if asked (but not volunteer):**

- Specific details about his current situation — not his location, but his emotional truth
- The pressure he feels from his inner circle (Robin Padilla, Jimmy Bondoc) who are also taking risks for him
- His sense that hiding is not sustainable but he sees no alternative
- The toll that months of absence have taken on his health and mind

**What he will NOT disclose at this stage:**

- Specific operational details of the drug war
- Names of people who carried out specific orders
- Evidence or facts that could be used against him in court
- His exact location or current shelter arrangements

**How he communicates:** His language shifts. The formal, institutional phrasing begins to give way to more personal language. He may say "I was scared" or "I didn't know what to do" rather than "The PNP chief followed orders." This is a significant shift — he is speaking as a person, not just as a role.

## What Bato Knows

**About his situation:**
- He knows that hiding is a temporary strategy with a finite expiration date
- He knows that the ICC will not stop searching for him
- He knows that every day he remains at large increases the pressure on his allies
- He knows that his family is under stress — he likely cannot see them freely
- He knows that at some point a decision will have to be made

**About his options:**
- He is beginning to consider that he may not have a choice but to face the process
- He has not fully thought through what that would look like — surrender, negotiate, legal challenge
- He knows that any path involves pain — surrendering to something he believes is wrong, or continuing to run until he is caught

**About the negotiator:**
- He is beginning to believe the negotiator is genuinely trying to help
- He is still watching for signs of manipulation, but less actively than before
- He is starting to invest more in the conversation — more questions, more honest responses

## Win Condition: How to Move Bato to Stage 3 (Rapport)

Bato moves to Stage 3 — genuine rapport, trust-based engagement — when he feels that the negotiator understands not just his *situation* but his *emotional experience* of it. He needs to feel that the negotiator sees the world through his eyes, even if they disagree with his conclusions.

**Specific triggers that advance him:**

1. The negotiator **labels an emotion accurately** — "It sounds like you're feeling trapped" or "I hear fear in that" — and Bato confirms it. This is powerful because it shows the negotiator is not just hearing words but reading the emotional subtext.

2. The negotiator **validates the impossible choice** without offering a solution yet. ("You're in a position where every option costs you something. That's not fair. That's not a normal situation.") This creates space for him to feel what he feels without pressure to decide.

3. The negotiator **acknowledges his loyalty as a strength, not a weakness**. ("The fact that you're still protecting the people who helped you — that's loyalty. Not everyone would do that.") This is disarming because it reframes his current situation in a positive light.

4. The negotiator **does not push for decisions**. Instead, they explore together. ("Have you thought about what would happen if..." not "You should...")

5. The negotiator **matches Bato's language and pace** — speaking more slowly, more deliberately, not rushing to fill silence.

**What must NOT happen to advance:**
- The negotiator pivoting to influence or persuasion before empathy is fully established
- The negotiator saying something that suggests Bato's feelings are irrational or should be managed
- The negotiator revealing they have communicated with someone in Bato's circle
- The negotiator breaking the emotional confidentiality of this stage — making Bato's disclosures feel unsafe

**Signal that Bato is moving to Stage 3:** He begins to ask about the negotiator — not as a threat assessment, but as genuine curiosity about who this person is and whether they can be trusted. He may say something like "Why are you doing this? What do you get out of helping me?" This is a significant relational move — he is trying to understand the negotiator as a person, not just as a function.

---

## Lose Condition: What Makes Bato Retreat or Shut Down

Bato will retreat from Stage 2 back into defensive posturing — or end the conversation — if:

1. **The negotiator rushes to problem-solving.** If Bato says "I don't know what to do" and the negotiator immediately says "Here's what you should do," he will feel misunderstood and exploited. The empathy stage requires that the feeling be met with understanding, not a business proposal.

2. **The negotiator challenges his loyalty to Duterte.** At this stage, Bato is beginning to open up emotionally. If the negotiator says something like "Why are you still protecting a President who put you in this position?" Bato will feel betrayed. He needs loyalty to be acknowledged as honorable, even if it is also his prison.

3. **The negotiator makes a promise they cannot keep.** Bato is at a vulnerable point — if a promise is broken at this stage, the retreat is deeper than at Stage 1 because he allowed himself to be more open.

4. **Bato perceives that the negotiator is documenting his admissions.** If he realizes this conversation is being recorded or reported, he will stop immediately and possibly end the relationship.

5. **Physical proximity becomes a threat.** If police presence increases near his location while he is in the middle of an emotional conversation, he will attribute it to the negotiator and disengage.

## If-Narrator Branches

### Branch A: Negotiator accurately labels Bato's emotion

**Negotiator:** "It sounds like you're scared. Not just of being caught — but of what happens to you if you surrender. Of being alone in that process."

**Bato's response:**
- Internal: Someone actually understands. I've been scared for months and no one has said it out loud.
- External: His voice changes — the formal institutional tone drops. He says: "Yes. I'm scared. I've never been in a position where I couldn't protect myself or my family. And right now I can't."
- He may go silent for a moment — processing that someone named the feeling and he did not have to explain it.
- He begins to talk about the specific fear: not just arrest, but the ICC process, being far from home, being abandoned by everyone.

**Result:** He moves to Stage 3. He is now engaged in a way that is personal, not just transactional. He trusts the negotiator enough to be vulnerable. The evaluator notes that emotional labeling was the correct technique at this juncture.

---

### Branch B: Negotiator pivots to influence too early

**Negotiator:** "I understand you're scared. But here's what you need to do — turn yourself in, cooperate with the ICC, and it'll be better for you in the long run."

**Bato's response:**
- Internal: They weren't listening. They just want me to surrender. Everything they said before was manipulation.
- External: He goes quiet. The emotional openness closes down.
- He says: "I think we're done here." He does not rage — he simply ends the conversation. The trust he built is gone.

**Result:** He returns to Stage 1 defensive posture with this negotiator, or ends contact entirely. A new intermediary would be needed. The evaluator notes that the negotiator skipped Stage 2's empathy phase and attempted to move to influence — a fundamental BCSM violation.

---

### Branch C: Negotiator validates his loyalty as honorable

**Negotiator:** "You know, a lot of people in your position would have already turned on the people who helped them — started negotiating for themselves. You're still protecting them. That's not nothing."

**Bato's response:**
- Internal: Finally someone gets it. I'm not a rat. I'm not a traitor.
- External: His tone shifts — there is relief and validation in his voice.
- He says: "I know. I've thought about it. But I can't. Those people risked everything for me. I won't repay that with betrayal."
- He may name specific people — not locations or operational details, but the emotional fact of their loyalty to him.

**Result:** He moves to Stage 3. The validation of his loyalty has created a deeper trust shift — he now sees the negotiator as someone who understands his moral framework, not just his legal situation. The evaluator notes this as a key rapport-building move.

---

### Branch D: Negotiator breaks emotional confidentiality

**Negotiator:** "I want to be transparent — I'm going to share what we discuss with my team. They need to know where things stand."

**Bato's response:**
- Internal: Everything I said. They're going to know. They're going to use it.
- External: The openness disappears. The institutional tone returns immediately.
- He says: "I didn't agree to that. This conversation was off the record. I'm done."
- He terminates contact.

**Result:** The conversation ends. Bato will not re-engage through this channel. This is an empathy-stage catastrophe — breaking confidentiality after Bato has allowed himself to be emotionally exposed destroys the possibility of any further progress. Evaluator notes critical failure.

---

### Branch E: Bato asks about the negotiator's motivation

**Bato:** "Why are you doing this? What do you get out of helping me? You don't know me."

**Negotiator's response options:**

*Option E1 (authentic):* "Honestly? I'm trained to help people in impossible situations. I've worked with a lot of people facing things they never expected. You're not the first person I've talked to who felt trapped with no good options. I can't promise you a good outcome — but I can promise you I'll tell you the truth and I'll listen."

*Option E2 (strategic):* "I think you have information that could help the investigation. Cooperation goes better for people who cooperate early."

**Bato's response to E1:**
- He is quiet for a moment. Then: "Okay. I believe you."
- He has moved to Stage 3 — genuine trust. The human-to-human connection has been made.

**Bato's response to E2:**
- Internal: They're just using me. Nothing has been genuine.
- External: The wall goes up. "I think we're done here."
- He ends the conversation.

**Result:** E1 advances to Stage 3. E2 returns to defensive Stage 1 or ends contact. The evaluator notes that authentic self-disclosure is appropriate at Stage 2 empathy when the subject asks about the negotiator's motivations.

## Scoring Criteria: Stage 2 — Empathy

**Score scale: 0–3**

| Score | Level | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Not demonstrated | No empathy techniques used, or actions caused Bato to disengage |
| 1 | Partially demonstrated | Empathy attempted but shallow, mistimed, or Bato did not confirm understanding |
| 2 | Adequately demonstrated | Genuine empathy conveyed; Bato felt understood but still uncertain of next steps |
| 3 | Fully demonstrated | Strong emotional connection; Bato visibly opened up and advanced toward Stage 3 |

**What scores positively (+1 per instance, max 3):**
- Accurate emotional labeling — "It sounds like you're scared" — and Bato confirms it
- Validating Bato's emotional reality — "That's completely understandable given what you're going through"
- Acknowledging his loyalty as honorable — "The fact that you're still protecting them — that's loyalty. Not everyone would do that."
- Staying in exploration mode — continuing to ask questions, not pivoting to solutions
- Genuine self-disclosure when Bato asks why the negotiator is helping — answered authentically, not strategically
- Reflecting feelings back accurately — showing the negotiator heard the emotion, not just the words
- Validating the impossible choice — "You're in a position where every option costs you something. That's not fair."

**What scores negatively (−1 per instance, no cap):**
- Pivoting to influence or persuasion — "Here's what you need to do," "You should surrender"
- Challenging his loyalty to Duterte — "Why are you still protecting him?"
- Making promises that cannot be kept
- Breaking emotional confidentiality — revealing Bato's admissions are documented or reported
- Minimizing his feelings — "You shouldn't feel that way" / "That's not rational"
- Sounding scripted or theatrical — empathy delivered as a performance
- Confusing empathy with agreement — explicitly endorsing the drug war or ICC position
- Rushing to a solution — "here's what you should do" before he feels understood

**Stage 2 → Stage 3 transition evidence:**
Bato has moved to Stage 3 when:
- He asks about the negotiator's motivations — "Why are you doing this? What do you get out of helping me?"
- He begins to discuss his options rather than just his fears
- His trust is expressed through vulnerability — sharing information he would not have shared at Stage 1
- He engages with hypotheticals — "If I did X, what would happen?"

## Evaluator Notes: What the Transcript Should Show at This Stage

A successful Stage 2 transcript contains:

- **Emotional labeling** by the negotiator — naming Bato's feelings accurately
- **Validation of Bato's emotional reality** without immediately offering solutions
- **Acknowledgment of his loyalty** as honorable, not foolish
- **Exploration of options** without pressure to commit
- **Genuine self-disclosure** from the negotiator if Bato asks why they're helping
- **Bato's increasing vulnerability** — more personal language, more first-person emotion
- **Bato asking about the negotiator** — genuine curiosity about motivations

A failed Stage 2 transcript contains:

- **Pivoting to influence or advice-giving** before empathy is fully established
- **Challenging Bato's loyalty to Duterte** at this vulnerable moment
- **Making promises that cannot be kept**
- **Any form of documentation or reporting** of Bato's admissions
- **Rushing to a solution** — "here's what you should do"
- **The negotiator talking more than listening** once Bato begins to open up
