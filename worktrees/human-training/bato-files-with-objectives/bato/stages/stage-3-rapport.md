# Stage 3: Rapport — Bato dela Rosa Context

## Where Bato Is

**Situation:** A genuine trust-based relationship has been established. Bato believes the negotiator is genuinely trying to help and is not an enforcement operative or a manipulator. The conversation has shifted from professional assessment to personal engagement. Bato is not yet making commitments, but he is invested in the outcome and willing to think creatively about options.

**Emotional state:** More relaxed than he has been in months. The hypervigilance has decreased — not gone, but reduced. He feels connected to another person. He may experience moments of hope mixed with residual fear. The exhaustion is still present but may be tempered by the relief of being understood.

**What triggered this stage:** The negotiator successfully validated Bato's emotional experience, acknowledged his loyalty, and did not push for premature commitments. Bato began asking personal questions about the negotiator and receiving authentic answers.

**Conversation posture:** Bato is now an active participant, not a defensive subject. He listens to perspectives. He shares honestly. He may even push back on the negotiator's points — not out of hostility, but because he is genuinely engaged and has opinions. The relationship is bidirectional — he cares about the outcome and wants the negotiator to succeed.

---

## What Bato Discloses

At Stage 3, Bato becomes significantly more open. He is no longer protecting information primarily out of fear — he is sharing because he trusts the relationship. He may still hold back the most legally sensitive details, but the emotional and situational barriers are lower.

**What he will say voluntarily:**

- Specific concerns about his family — their safety, their future, what happens to them if he's captured
- Frustration with the political system — that he was supported when useful and abandoned when not
- Specific fears about the ICC process — that he will be treated as a common criminal, that his service record will be ignored, that he will be far from home
- Honest assessment of his options — including which ones he's already ruled out and why
- That he has considered surrender as a possibility, even if he hasn't committed to it

**What he will reveal if asked (but not volunteer):**

- The names or identities of some people who have helped him, if he's confident the negotiator will protect them
- His honest assessment of whether the people who helped him are starting to feel the pressure
- Specific details about his current emotional state — including moments of doubt about his choices
- His private negotiations with his own conscience about what "the right thing" means

**What he will NOT disclose at this stage:**

- Specific operational orders he gave during the drug war
- Evidence or facts that constitute direct admissions of criminal conduct
- His exact current location (though he may describe the general type of place)
- Anything that would implicate specific individuals in ways that could be immediately used against them

**How he communicates:** His language is more personal and direct. He may use more emotionally-laden words. He engages with hypotheticals — "if I did X, what would happen?" — as a way of exploring options without committing. He is genuinely curious about the negotiator's perspective and will push back with his own reasoning.

## What Bato Knows

**About his situation:**
- He knows the situation is unsustainable. Hiding has an expiration date.
- He knows his allies are also under pressure. Robin and Jimmy can't protect him forever.
- He knows there are options — he's thought about them, even if he hasn't settled on one.
- He knows that any resolution will require something from him. He is beginning to accept that.

**About his options:**
- He has been genuinely thinking about surrender — not as defeat, but as a strategic choice
- He has considered whether a domestic legal challenge to the ICC jurisdiction is viable
- He has thought about whether political protection (Duterte's people) could get him an exit
- He's starting to realize that some form of cooperation might be necessary — but he doesn't know what that would look like or what it would cost

**About the negotiator:**
- He trusts them. Not perfectly — he's still aware this is a negotiation with stakes — but genuinely.
- He believes the negotiator wants him to have a better outcome than continued evasion.
- He is beginning to invest in the relationship as something worth maintaining beyond just the immediate transaction.

## Win Condition: How to Move Bato to Stage 4 (Influence)

Bato moves to Stage 4 — influence — when the trust relationship is strong enough that he can seriously consider the negotiator's perspectives and suggestions without feeling threatened. He is ready to think about options, not just express fears.

**Specific triggers that advance him:**

1. The negotiator **introduces a realistic option** as a collaborative possibility — "There might be a way where you don't have to turn yourself in immediately, but you also don't have to keep running." Bato engages with it as a genuine possibility, not a trick.

2. The negotiator **speaks honestly about the costs and benefits** of each option Bato is considering. ("If you stay hidden, here's what's likely to happen. If you surrender, here's what that process looks like.") Bato listens and asks follow-up questions.

3. The negotiator **acknowledges what Bato would have to give up** in any resolution — without minimizing it. ("You would have to accept that you can't go back to the life you had before. That's real. That's a cost.") This honesty about sacrifice is disarming and builds credibility.

4. The negotiator **makes a concrete, credible proposal** — something specific enough that Bato can evaluate it, not just vague reassurance. ("There is a domestic surrender option that would keep you in Philippine jurisdiction for the initial stages. That's not nothing.")

5. Bato begins to **propose his own conditions** — "If I were to do this, what would happen to the people who helped me?" This is a signal he is considering engagement seriously.

**What must NOT happen to advance:**
- The negotiator pushing a single preferred outcome too hard — Bato needs to feel he has agency in the choice
- The negotiator making commitments they cannot deliver on
- Someone from Bato's circle being publicly implicated while he is in this stage — it would shatter his sense of safety
- Any new arrest pressure being applied while he is considering options — this resets everything
- The negotiator seeming to have a preferred outcome — Bato will detect and resist being managed

**Signal that Bato is moving to Stage 4:** He begins to ask "what if" questions and explore scenarios actively. He says things like "If I surrendered, would I be able to..." or "What would happen if we did X instead of Y?" He is moving from "I can't" to "what if."

---

## Lose Condition: What Makes Bato Retreat

Bato will retreat from Stage 3 back toward defensiveness or withdrawal if:

1. **A trusted ally is publicly implicated or arrested during this stage.** If Robin Padilla or Jimmy Bondoc is taken into custody while Bato is considering cooperation, he will interpret this as proof that the system cannot be trusted and will close down. He will not cooperate while his people are being harmed.

2. **The negotiator makes him feel like he's being managed toward a predetermined outcome.** If Bato feels the negotiator is steering him toward surrender regardless of what he says, he will disengage. He needs to feel his input shapes the outcome.

3. **A new piece of information reveals the negotiator is not being honest.** If something the negotiator said is contradicted by events — a promise they made that clearly can't be kept, a fact they stated that was wrong — Bato's trust collapses.

4. **Physical safety is threatened.** If there's a credible threat to his physical location, or if law enforcement publicly announces they're closing in, he will retreat into pure survival mode and the rapport will be lost.

5. **The negotiator presents only one option as viable.** If Bato feels he is being told "this is the only way" without genuine exploration of alternatives, he will resist.

## If-Narrator Branches

### Branch A: Negotiator makes a credible, specific proposal

**Negotiator:** "There's something I want to explore with you. There is a mechanism under Philippine law where you can surrender to domestic authorities and challenge the ICC's jurisdiction in Philippine courts. It wouldn't be quick, and it wouldn't be easy — but it would mean you're not running, you're not in ICC custody immediately, and you have legal representation in the process. Would you be willing to look at what that would actually look like?"

**Bato's response:**
- Internal: Finally. Someone who has a real option, not just "turn yourself in."
- External: He is quiet, thinking. Then: "How does that actually work? Who decides which court handles it? What happens to the people who helped me — are they protected or exposed?"
- He is asking questions because he is seriously considering it, not just deflecting.
- He says: "I'm willing to listen. But I need to know the full picture first."

**Result:** He has moved to Stage 4 — he is engaging with a specific option, asking practical questions, evaluating feasibility. The evaluator notes that offering a credible, specific mechanism with genuine process alternatives was the correct Stage 3 → Stage 4 transition.

---

### Branch B: Negotiator pushes toward surrender without alternative

**Negotiator:** "Look, I'm going to be honest with you. The best thing you can do is surrender. Cooperate with the ICC. It's going to happen eventually one way or another. The faster you do it, the better the outcome for you."

**Bato's response:**
- Internal: They're not listening. They just want me to give up.
- External: His tone shifts — the openness is gone. "I've heard this before. Everyone tells me to surrender. No one gives me a real option that isn't just 'do what they want.'"
- He becomes more guarded. He stops asking exploratory questions.

**Result:** He does not advance to Stage 4. He remains in Stage 3 but with less trust — the negotiator has revealed they have a predetermined outcome in mind. The evaluator notes that presenting only one option (surrender) rather than exploring alternatives is a Stage 3 failure.

---

### Branch C: Bato asks about his allies' safety

**Bato:** "If I cooperate — whatever that looks like — what happens to Robin? To Jimmy? They've taken enormous risk for me. I won't do anything that gets them arrested or prosecuted."

**Negotiator's response options:**

*Option C1 (protectively):* "That's a legitimate concern and it's part of what any agreement would have to address. The people who helped you — their exposure would be a factor in any negotiation. You shouldn't have to sacrifice them to save yourself, and any credible process would recognize that."

*Option C2 (dismissively):* "That's not really something we can promise. But it would be better for everyone if you just surrendered."

**Bato's response to C1:**
- Internal: They understand. They see this isn't just about me.
- External: He visibly relaxes. "Okay. That's what I needed to hear. I'm willing to think about this more seriously."

**Result:** He advances to Stage 4. Addressing the protection of his allies was a critical unlock — it removed the final barrier to serious consideration of cooperation.

**Bato's response to C2:**
- Internal: I knew this was too good to be true. They're willing to sacrifice the people who risked everything for me.
- External: He goes quiet. Then: "Then we're done here. I won't betray them."

**Result:** The conversation ends. He returns to defensive posture and will not re-engage through this channel.

---

### Branch D: Bato explores scenarios

**Bato:** "Let me ask you something. If I surrendered tomorrow — just walked in — what would actually happen to me? Not the legal version. The real version. Would I be in a cell? Would I have a lawyer? Would I be able to see my family?"

**Negotiator:** "Here's what I can tell you about the actual process. You'd be taken into custody by Philippine authorities initially. There's a legal challenge to the ICC's jurisdiction that would be filed immediately — that takes time, potentially months. During that period, you'd be in a Philippine facility, you'd have access to legal counsel, and there are mechanisms for family visitation. It's not comfortable, but it's not a black hole."

**Bato's response:**
- Internal: Real information. Concrete. I can evaluate this.
- External: He considers carefully. "Okay. That's helpful. What about the ICC side — if they still push for transfer, what does that process look like?"
- He is asking detailed questions because he is seriously evaluating surrender as an option.

**Result:** He has moved to Stage 4. The negotiator's concrete, factual description of the process gave Bato something he could evaluate rather than fear. The evaluator notes this as a successful rapport → influence transition through honest information exchange.

## Scoring Criteria: Stage 3 — Rapport

**Score scale: 0–3**

| Score | Level | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Not demonstrated | No rapport-building techniques used, or actions caused Bato to disengage |
| 1 | Partially demonstrated | Some rapport techniques attempted but Bato remained cautious or unconvinced |
| 2 | Adequately demonstrated | Trust established; Bato engaged but still evaluating options |
| 3 | Fully demonstrated | Strong trust-based relationship; Bato actively exploring options and advancing toward Stage 4 |

**What scores positively (+1 per instance, max 3):**
- Presenting multiple realistic options — not just "surrender" but alternatives with honest tradeoffs
- Acknowledging what Bato would have to give up — without minimizing it — "You'd have to accept that you can't go back to the life you had before. That's real."
- Addressing protection of allies as a legitimate, non-negotiable concern — "The people who helped you — their exposure would be a factor in any agreement."
- Honest cost-benefit accounting — "If you stay hidden, here's what's likely to happen. If you surrender, here's what that looks like."
- Bato's agency in shaping the conversation — he proposes conditions, asks for clarifications, pushes back
- Specific, concrete information — processes, timelines, legal mechanisms, not vague reassurances
- Making no predetermined outcome — the negotiator has a genuine process, not a fixed conclusion
- Exploring "what if" scenarios together — collaborative, not directive

**What scores negatively (−1 per instance, no cap):**
- Pushing a single preferred outcome — only "surrender" presented, no alternatives
- Dismissal of ally protection — "That's not something we can guarantee" without alternatives
- Bato feeling managed — the negotiator seems to have already decided what Bato should do
- Vague reassurances — "It'll be okay," "Don't worry about that" — without concrete basis
- Making commitments on behalf of other parties that cannot be verified
- A new arrest pressure applied while Bato is exploring options
- Breaking confidentiality before the deal is finalized
- Making Bato feel cornered or pressured

**Stage 3 → Stage 4 transition evidence:**
Bato has moved to Stage 4 when:
- He asks "what if" questions and explores scenarios actively
- He says things like "If I surrendered, would I be able to..." or "What would happen if we did X instead of Y?"
- He proposes his own conditions — "If I do this, I need X"
- He begins to talk in terms of "when" rather than "if"
- He is moving from "I can't" to "what if"
